I

PROFILE

Background and engineering approach.

I build software that runs where downtime is measured in money.

For the past two years I have developed HMI software at Kaban Makina, a heavy machinery manufacturer. My responsibility is the full-screen operator interface: the C# application that turns a job order into machine motion, and machine state into something a shift operator can read at a glance. Incorrect information on that screen produces scrap; an unresponsive screen stops a production line.

Before that I spent two years as a C# engineer at a foreign exchange firm, on systems that had to remain correct for as long as the market was open: live price distribution, order entry and routing, execution handling and transaction processing — built on a microservice architecture and exercised continuously against a non-production mirror.

The domains are different; the engineering discipline is not — deterministic behaviour, honest state, thorough logging, and systems that degrade predictably rather than surprisingly.

OPERATING PRINCIPLES

  • 01If the operator made a mistake, the interface allowed it.
  • 02A value on screen is a commitment: accurate, or not shown at all.
  • 03Log as though you will be debugging it at 3 a.m.
  • 04Deliver, then stay with the system while it runs in production.

III

CAPITAL MARKETS

Two years of C# on systems that had to stay correct while the market was open.

  1. PRICE DISTRIBUTIONLive quote ingestion and distribution. A stale tick is an incorrect tick, so freshness was treated as a correctness requirement rather than a performance one.
  2. ORDER ENTRYBuy and sell instructions accepted, validated and routed, with the constraint checks that keep an erroneous order from reaching the market.
  3. EXECUTIONFills, partial fills and rejections handled as expected outcomes with defined state transitions, not as exceptions.
  4. TRANSACTION PROCESSINGTransactions carried through to a final, reconcilable state with a complete audit trail.

PRODUCTION AND MIRROR

The production environment ran alongside a non-production mirror on the same build, fed both live and replayed flow, so no release reached customers without first being exercised under realistic conditions.

MICROSERVICE ARCHITECTURE

Pricing, order handling and processing were separated into independently deployable services, each able to fail, restart and scale on its own — which contains the impact of any single fault.

WHAT IT ESTABLISHED

Latency as a requirement rather than an optimisation, idempotency as a default, and a working understanding of ordering and time in distributed systems.
